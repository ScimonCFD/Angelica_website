Non-isothermal Solver

Heat transport on a converged flow field.

The hydraulic solver provides the converged pressure field and mass flow rates. The thermal solver uses that result to solve the steady energy equation, updates temperature-dependent fluid properties, and iterates until both the flow field and temperature field are mutually consistent. Density and viscosity depend on temperature; the flow field depends on both. Angelica resolves this coupling through a segregated outer loop — SIMPLE runs to full convergence on every iteration, then the energy equation updates the temperature field. Under-relaxation ($\alpha \in (0,\,1]$, default $\alpha = 1$) damps the temperature update on each outer iteration; reducing $\alpha$ below 1 helps looped networks where the thermal–hydraulic coupling is stronger.

Solver framework

Where the thermal solver fits.

The non-isothermal solver activates all six steps. Temperature-dependent fluid properties are evaluated at step 3, the energy equation is solved at step 5, and the outer loop at step 6 iterates until the temperature field converges. Steps 4 and 5 are documented on their own pages — click either block to navigate.

Applicability

Built for incompressible liquids.

The thermal solver extends the isothermal hydraulic solver for cases where temperature varies along the network and fluid properties depend on temperature. Its assumptions match those of real district heating, heat-exchanger, and pipeline systems where the fluid is a liquid under moderate pressure.

Valid for

Steady-state flow in incompressible liquids — water, thermal oils, crude oil — where density $\rho$, viscosity $\mu$, specific heat $c_p$, and thermal conductivity $k$ depend on temperature but not on pressure. Property updates are evaluated at the local temperature on each outer iteration.

Not valid for

Compressible gases, where $\rho = \rho(P, T)$ — the pressure effect on density is not modelled. Transient effects (thermal capacitance, hydraulic inertia) are also not covered. For those cases a transient compressible solver is required.

Governing equation

Steady 1-D energy balance along each pipe.

For a fluid element moving along a pipe at mass flow rate $\dot{m}$, the steady-state energy balance per unit length is:

$$\dot{m}\,c_p\,\frac{dT}{dx} = -U\pi D\,(T - T_{\!\text{amb}}) + q'$$

$\dot{m}$ — mass flow rate (kg s−1)  ·  $c_p$ — specific heat (J kg−1 K−1)  ·  $T$ — fluid temperature  ·  $T_{\!\text{amb}}$ — ambient temperature  ·  $U$ — overall heat transfer coefficient (W m−2 K−1)  ·  $D$ — pipe inner diameter  ·  $q'$ — heat source per unit length (W m−1)

Sign convention: $U > 0$ means heat is lost to the environment; $q' > 0$ means heat is added to the fluid by an inline heater. Both terms can coexist on the same element — a heated pipe with wall losses is one example.

The mass flow rate $\dot{m}$ on the right side comes directly from the SIMPLE hydraulic solve. Once the hydraulic solver converges, $\dot{m}$ is fixed for this energy solve — the equation is then linear in $T$ and can be solved exactly.

Pipe heat loss

Exact analytical solution — no numerical diffusion.

NTU analytical solution

For a pipe with no internal source ($q' = 0$), the governing ODE has the exact closed-form solution:

$$T_{\text{out}} = T_{\!\text{amb}} + (T_{\text{in}} - T_{\!\text{amb}})\,e^{-\text{NTU}}$$

$\text{NTU} = \dfrac{U\pi D L}{\dot{m}\,c_p}$   — Number of Transfer Units

Angelica applies this formula element-by-element in a sweep from the pipe inlet to the outlet. Each pipe is divided into $N$ internal segments of equal length; the NTU formula is applied to each segment using the segment outlet as the next segment's inlet.

This approach is exact in the limit $N \to \infty$ and avoids the numerical diffusion that a finite-difference discretisation of the convective derivative would introduce.

Validation — analytical and textbook benchmarks

NTU exact formula

A symmetric looped network (L = 500 m, D = 25 mm, U = 50 W/m²K, ṁ = 1 kg/s, Tin = 80 °C, Tamb = 20 °C) gives NTU = 0.470 and an exact outlet temperature of 57.5 °C. Angelica reproduces this within 0.3 K.

Cengel & Ghajar Example 8-3 [9]

Dead crude oil at 20 °C flowing through a 200 m pipe submerged in an icy lake (0 °C). Textbook result: Texit = 19.74 °C. Angelica reproduces this within 0.05 K using the same convective coefficient h = 16.3 W/m²K.

Inline heaters and coolers

Fixed thermal power — source term linearisation.

An inline heater or cooler delivers a fixed total thermal power $Q$ (W) uniformly distributed over $n$ internal nodes. Following Moukalled, Mangani & Darwish [2], the source term is linearised as:

$$S_u = S_c + S_p\,T_P, \qquad S_c = \frac{Q}{n},\quad S_p = 0$$

$S_c$ — constant part of the source (W node−1)  ·  $S_p \le 0$ — linearised coefficient (ensures diagonal dominance)  ·  $T_P$ — node temperature

Since $S_p = 0$, the linearisation is exact for a fixed-power device: the power delivered does not depend on the local temperature, so no coefficient is needed. The total energy added is always exactly $Q$, regardless of the number of internal nodes ($S_c \times n = Q$). A cooler is represented by $Q < 0$.

Hydraulic effect of heaters

In the hydraulic solver, a heater is treated as a passive element with a user-specified pressure drop. It does not add energy to the momentum equation — only to the energy equation. This separation means the hydraulic and thermal effects can be validated independently.

Validation — energy balance

For a heater adding $Q = 50\,000\ \text{W}$ to a flow of $\dot{m} = 1\ \text{kg/s}$ with $c_p = 4182\ \text{J/kg\,K}$, the exact energy balance gives:

$$\Delta T = \frac{Q}{\dot{m}\,c_p} = \frac{50\,000}{1 \times 4182} = 11.96\ \text{K}$$

Angelica reproduces this within 0.2 K.
