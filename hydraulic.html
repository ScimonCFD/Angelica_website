Hydraulic Solver  —  Step 4 of the solver framework

The hydraulic loop at the core of Angelica.

Given the current temperature field, this solver computes the converged pressure field and mass flow rates using a modified SIMPLE pressure-correction algorithm (Patankar, 1980 [4]), applied to networks of pipes, fittings, and pumps.

Solver framework

Where the hydraulic solver fits.

In the isothermal solver, steps 1, 3, 5, and 6 are trivial or skipped entirely. Temperature is uniform and never updated, fluid properties are constants from user input, and no energy equation is solved. Only the hydraulic loop at step 4 executes in full.

Governing equations

Two equations govern the hydraulic problem.

Momentum — energy equation per pipe

Relates the pressure drop between two nodes to elevation change and friction losses along each link.

$$(P_{in} - P_{out}) = \rho g\,(z_{out} - z_{in}) + f\,\frac{l}{D}\,\frac{\rho V^2}{2}$$

$D$ — internal diameter  ·  $l$ — pipe length  ·  $f$ — friction factor  ·  $\rho$ — fluid density  ·  $z$ — elevation  ·  $V$ — mean velocity

In laminar flow $f = 64/Re$ where $Re = \rho V D / \mu$, reducing this to the linear Poiseuille relation. In turbulent flow, $f$ is a function of $Re$ and the relative wall roughness $\varepsilon/D$ — evaluated at each iteration via a closure relation (Colebrook-White).

Continuity — mass conservation at each node

Mass is conserved at every junction. The pressure field is driven by this equation — the pressure-correction algorithm is derived directly from it, and its residual $b_C$ measures how far the current iterate is from the solution.

$$\sum_{in}\dot{m}_{in} \;=\; \sum_{out}\dot{m}_{out}$$

These two equations are solved together. Even though Angelica uses a segregated pressure-correction algorithm, the converged solution satisfies both simultaneously at every node and every link.

The algorithm

Modified SIMPLE — laminar seed, nonlinear solve.

The SIMPLE loop runs in two stages. Stage 1 assumes laminar flow everywhere and converges in a single iteration for pure-pipe networks — producing an exact initial field. Stage 2 uses that field to seed the nonlinear solve with Darcy-Weisbach friction, Colebrook-White closure, fittings, and pumps.

Calculating V*

In this implementation, the provisional velocity field $V^*$ is computed from the current pressure field according to the active element law. The form of $V^*$ therefore depends on the regime and on the constitutive equation selected for each element type.

Laminar pipes

In Stage 1, pipe velocity is obtained from the linear laminar relation, including the elevation term. This is the Poiseuille-style form used to build the exact laminar seed.

$$V^* = \frac{D^2}{32\mu L}\left(\Delta P - \rho g \Delta z\right)$$

Turbulent pipes

In Stage 2, the Darcy-Weisbach equation is inverted with the friction factor $f^*$ frozen at its current iterate. Because $f^*$ depends on $Re^* = \rho|V^*|D/\mu$, $V^*$ and $f^*$ are resolved together by the two nested loops described in the Colebrook-White closure section below.

$$V^* = \operatorname{sign}(\Delta P - \rho g\,\Delta z) \sqrt{\frac{2D\,|\Delta P - \rho g\,\Delta z|}{\rho\,f^*\!L}}$$

Fittings and local-loss devices

For fittings, $V^*$ is computed from the local-loss relation using the fitting loss coefficient $K_L$. The preset fitting coefficients currently included in Angelica follow the values proposed in Munson.

$$V^* = \operatorname{sign}(\Delta P)\sqrt{\frac{2|\Delta P|}{K_L \rho}}$$

Pumps

For pumps, the current pressure difference is converted into the head the pump must supply. The pump curve then returns the corresponding flow rate, which is converted to $V^*$ through the element area.

$$H_{\mathrm{req}} = -\frac{\Delta P}{\rho g}, \qquad Q = G(H_{\mathrm{req}}), \qquad V^* = \frac{Q}{A}$$

Under-relaxation

After each pressure-correction step, the new pressure field is applied as:

$$P^{\,\text{new}} = P^* + \alpha\,P'$$

where $\alpha \in (0,\,1]$ is the pressure relaxation factor. With $\alpha = 1$ the full correction is applied — this is the default and works well for most networks. Reducing $\alpha$ below 1 damps the update, which improves stability for stiff cases such as networks with pumps operating near their shut-off head or networks with steep pump curves. Typical values when relaxation is needed are $\alpha \approx 0.3$–$0.7$.

Turbulent closure — Colebrook-White [1]

The Darcy-Weisbach equation requires the friction factor $f$, which for turbulent flow depends on both the Reynolds number $Re$ and the relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$. The Colebrook-White relation gives $f$ implicitly:

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{f}} = -2\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{3.7\,D} + \frac{2.51}{Re\sqrt{f}}\right)$$

Because $f$ appears on both sides — and $Re$ itself depends on $V^*$ — the closure requires two nested loops at each SIMPLE step:

  • Inner loop — finds $f$ for a fixed $V^*$. Angelica solves on the log-transformed variable $\ln f$, which is more numerically stable than solving for $f$ directly. Default solver: Newton-Raphson.
  • Outer loop — finds $V^*$ consistent with $f(V^*)$, since updating $V^*$ changes $Re$ and therefore $f$. Default solver: fixed-point iteration.

Both loops accept Newton-Raphson, secant, or fixed-point as the nonlinear solver — selectable per run. At convergence of the outer SIMPLE loop, $V^*$, $Re$, and $f$ are all mutually consistent.

Hazen-Williams replaces Colebrook-White with an explicit power-law relation, so no inner iteration is needed — the outer algorithm structure is otherwise identical.

Convergence criteria

Each stage declares convergence when two conditions hold simultaneously.

1. Pressure-correction magnitude. After the pressure field is updated, the largest correction applied to any node must fall below a threshold:

$$\Delta P_{\max} = \max_i \lvert \alpha\,P'_i \rvert \;\leq\; \varepsilon_{\Delta P}$$

$P'_i$ is the raw correction at node $i$ and $\alpha$ is the relaxation factor, so $\Delta P_{\max}$ is the largest pressure change actually applied in that iteration. The default threshold is $\varepsilon_{\Delta P} = 10^{-3}\ \text{Pa}$. A relative criterion is checked in parallel — convergence is declared if either the absolute or the relative threshold is met.

2. Nodal mass imbalance. At every internal junction mass that enters must equal mass that leaves. Let $\dot{m}^+_j$ and $\dot{m}^-_j$ be the total mass flow into and out of junction $j$ (kg/s). The relative imbalance is:

$$e_j = \frac{\left\lvert \dot{m}^+_j - \dot{m}^-_j \right\rvert}{\dot{m}^+_j + \dot{m}^-_j}$$

Because numerator and denominator carry the same units (kg/s), $e_j$ is dimensionless and lies in $[0,\,1]$. Convergence requires the largest imbalance across all internal junctions to fall below the threshold:

$$\max_j\, e_j \;\leq\; \varepsilon_{\text{cont}}$$

Boundary nodes (known pressure or known flow) are excluded — their net flux is supplied or extracted by the boundary condition, not a numerical error. The default threshold is $\varepsilon_{\text{cont}} = 10^{-3}$ (−).

The iteration stops as soon as both criteria are satisfied. If neither is met within the iteration limit, the stage terminates and the solver reports the run as unconverged.

Pressure-correction derivation

How continuity becomes a correction equation.

The SIMPLE decomposition

The solver begins with an initial pressure field $P^*$. With $P^*$ fixed, each element's constitutive law — Poiseuille for laminar pipes, Darcy-Weisbach for turbulent, $K_L$ for fittings, or the pump curve — determines $V^*$ directly. $V^*$ is not guessed independently; it is the velocity that the element law prescribes for the current $P^*$. The true solution is then related to these estimates by additive corrections:

$$P = P^* + P', \qquad V = V^* + V'$$

Substituting into the continuity equation and rearranging produces a linear system in $P'$. The key step — and what distinguishes element types — is expressing $V'$ as a function of $P'$. For laminar flow this relation is exact. For nonlinear elements it requires an approximation that holds exactly at convergence.

Substituting into continuity

Consider node $C$ connected by two pipes, each with a control volume bounded by faces $w$ (west) and $e$ (east).

Replacing every $\dot{m}_f$ with $\dot{m}^*_f + \dot{m}'_f$ in the continuity equation at node $C$ and grouping known and unknown terms:

$$\underbrace{\sum_{\mathrm{in}} \dot{m}^*_f - \sum_{\mathrm{out}} \dot{m}^*_f}_{b_C} \;+\; \sum_{\mathrm{in}} \dot{m}'_f - \sum_{\mathrm{out}} \dot{m}'_f \;=\; 0$$

The first group is $b_C$ — the mass-flux residual computed from the current velocity estimate. The second group must cancel it, so the mass-flow corrections must satisfy:

$$\sum_{\mathrm{in}} \dot{m}'_f - \sum_{\mathrm{out}} \dot{m}'_f \;=\; -b_C$$

It remains to express each $\dot{m}'_f$ in terms of the unknown pressure corrections $P'$. Every element law relates $\dot{m}$ to the nodal pressure difference, so linearising around $V^*$ gives $\dot{m}'_f$ directly. For a laminar pipe the Poiseuille relation is linear in $\Delta P$ — the step is exact:

$$\dot{m}'_f \;=\; c_f\,(P'_F - P'_C), \qquad c_f = \frac{\rho A D^2}{32\,\mu\,l}$$

Substituting and collecting over all faces of node $C$, then defining $a_f = -c_f$ and $a_C = -\sum_f a_f$:

$$\sum_f c_f(P'_F - P'_C) = b_C \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; a_C\,P'_C + \sum_f a_f\,P'_F = b_C$$

where $a_f = -c_f$, $\;a_C = -\displaystyle\sum_f a_f$, and $\;b_C = \displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{in}}\dot{m}^*_f - \sum_{\mathrm{out}}\dot{m}^*_f$. For nonlinear elements the same structure holds but $c_f$ — and therefore $a_f$ — depends on $V^*$ and is updated every iteration. At convergence, $V^* \to V$ and the approximation becomes exact.

Laminar pipe — exact coefficient

The Poiseuille equation is linear in pressure, so $V'$ can be expressed exactly in terms of $P'$. The coefficient $c_f$ is constant for each pipe:

$$c_f = \frac{\rho A D^2}{32\,\mu\,l} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad a_f = -c_f$$

Because $a_f$ is constant, the pressure-correction system is linear and a network of purely laminar pipes converges in a single iteration [2].

Turbulent pipe — linearised coefficient

The turbulent momentum equation is nonlinear in $V$, so an exact $V'(P')$ relation does not exist. Using $V \approx V^*$ — which holds exactly at convergence:

$$a_f = -\!\left(\frac{2AD}{l\,f_{V^*}|V^*|}\right)_{\!f}$$

$f_{V^*}$ is the Colebrook-White friction factor evaluated at $V^*$, updated every iteration. As $V^* \to V$, the approximation becomes exact.

Fitting — loss coefficient

The pressure drop across a fitting is quadratic in velocity, governed by $K_L$. Applying the same linearisation:

$$\Delta P = K_L\,\frac{\rho V^2}{2} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad a_f = -\!\left(\frac{2A}{K_L|V^*|}\right)_{\!f}$$

The preset $K_L$ values built into Angelica are taken from Munson et al. [3].

Pump — curve-driven pressure changer

Pumps are treated as active pressure-changing devices. The current nodal pressure field defines the head the pump must supply:

$$H_{\mathrm{req}} = -\frac{\Delta P}{\rho g}$$

The pump curve then returns the corresponding flow rate, written locally as $Q = G(H_{\mathrm{req}})$. Angelica supports both single-point EPANET-style pump definitions and piecewise-linear multi-point $Q$-Head tables.

$$Q = G(H_{\mathrm{req}}), \qquad V = \frac{Q}{A}$$

For pressure correction, the pump contributes the local slope of the curve. This plays the same role as the hydraulic conductance of a pipe or fitting:

$$a_f \propto \frac{dQ}{d(\Delta P)} = \frac{dQ}{dH}\,\frac{1}{\rho g}$$

In other words, a pump enters the same nodal continuity system as every other element. What changes is only the local constitutive law used to relate pressure difference and flow.

Boundary conditions

Known pressure

Set $P' = 0$ at the boundary node. The prescribed pressure is already exact — no correction needed.

Known mass flow

The boundary flow is imposed directly. The $P'$ term for that node does not appear in the system — it is absorbed into $b_C$ of the connected interior nodes.

At least one pressure boundary is required for a unique solution. Mixed types — pressure sources, pressure sinks, flow inlets, flow outlets — can coexist in the same network.

Fluid properties

From field data to solver inputs.

The solver requires two fluid properties at every iteration: density $\rho$ and dynamic viscosity $\mu$. Angelica provides two ways to supply them.

General liquid — direct input

For water, refined products, or any single-phase liquid with known properties, density and viscosity are entered directly. The solver uses these values as constants throughout the simulation.

A water preset is provided at standard conditions ($\rho = 998.25\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$, $\mu = 1.002 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{Pa s}$).

Crude oil — Beggs & Robinson (1975) [8]

For dead crude oil the user provides two field parameters: API gravity and operating temperature. Density and viscosity are then computed automatically before the solver runs.

Density from API gravity

The API gravity scale converts to specific gravity and then to density at standard conditions:

$$SG = \frac{141.5}{API + 131.5}, \qquad \rho = SG \times 999.064\ \ \text{kg m}^{-3}$$

Viscosity — Beggs & Robinson correlation

Dead-oil viscosity is estimated from a double-logarithmic expression that correlates viscosity with API gravity and temperature in degrees Fahrenheit:

$$\log_{10}\!\bigl(\log_{10}(\mu_{\text{dead}} + 1)\bigr) = \underbrace{(3.0324 - 0.02023\cdot API)}_{A} - 1.163\,\log_{10}(T_{\!\circ F})$$

$\mu_{\text{dead}}$ — dead-oil viscosity (cP)  ·  $T_{\!\circ F}$ — temperature in °F  ·  $API$ — API gravity (°API)

The resulting viscosity in centipoise is converted to SI units ($\text{Pa s} = \text{cP} / 1000$) before being passed to the solver.

Isothermal use of temperature (crude oil mode)

In the crude oil property model, temperature enters only at this property-evaluation step. Once $\rho$ and $\mu$ are computed, the isothermal solver treats them as constants. This is physically consistent with the assumption that the pipeline operates at a uniform, known temperature (e.g. reservoir temperature or an insulated flowline average).

For applications where temperature varies along the network — district heating, heat exchangers, chilled water systems — the full non-isothermal solver described in the next section solves the energy equation on the converged flow field and handles temperature-dependent properties iteratively.

Example — 32°API at 65°C

$\rho = 864.6\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$

$\mu = 4.25\ \text{cP}\ \ (0.00425\ \text{Pa s})$

See Tutorial 11 for a full branched pipeline case using these values.

Validity range

The Beggs & Robinson correlation is applicable to dead crude oils in the range 16–58°API and temperatures above 0°F (−18°C).
